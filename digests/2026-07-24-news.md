Good morning. Three threads today: the AI industry took a visible turn toward unglamorous but lucrative problems this week, Uruguay's budget fight found its actual deciding vote, and a fresh round of attacks in the Red Sea just pushed oil past a price level it hasn't touched since May. Let's get into it.

Start with a stable release that matters more than it sounds like it should: the Chinese lab DeepSeek shipped a stable version of its V4 model, ending months of preview builds that had kept cautious enterprises from putting real production workloads on it. DeepSeek's pricing, roughly forty four cents per million output tokens, is already the number every other lab gets measured against when a client asks why a competitor's model costs so much more, and ending the preview-build churn matters in its own right, since a model that keeps changing under an enterprise's feet every few weeks is genuinely hard to build a stable production workflow on top of, no matter how good its benchmark scores look. Layered on top of that price pressure, a coding startup called eight zero nine zero Labs closed a hundred thirty five million dollar Series A led by Salesforce Ventures specifically to scale what it calls a Software Factory, an agentic coding system built for regulated industries like healthcare and aerospace, where a generic coding assistant with no awareness of compliance requirements, audit trails, or industry-specific validation rules is closer to a liability than a productivity tool. Put those two stories together and the pattern is consistent with everything this briefing has tracked for weeks: cheap, commoditized general-purpose models on one side, getting cheaper and more stable by the month, and increasingly specialized, compliance-aware tooling built on top of them on the other, with the real money and the real differentiation migrating toward that second category rather than the underlying model itself. If you are scoping a delivery team's AI tooling budget, that split is worth applying directly: treat the base model as a commodity input whose price you should expect to keep falling, and spend your actual evaluation effort on whichever specialized layer sits on top of it for your specific industry.

That specialization trend showed up again, in a more surprising place, when the German enterprise software giant SAP completed its acquisition of Prior Labs, a research lab that pioneered what is called a tabular foundation model, meaning a model purpose-built for the structured rows-and-columns data that actually runs most businesses, rather than for text or images. SAP is committing more than a billion euros over the next four years to grow Prior Labs into what it is calling a frontier AI lab focused entirely on that structured-data problem, and Prior Labs will keep operating as an independent entity inside that arrangement rather than being folded straight into SAP's existing product lines. The practical use cases are unglamorous in exactly the way that makes them valuable: predicting which customers are about to churn, which suppliers are at risk of missing a delivery, and which invoices are likely to pay late, using the same kind of foundation-model approach that made large language models good at text, just aimed at spreadsheets and databases instead of prose. SAP's own framing of the deal is telling: it describes tabular data, the ordinary rows and columns sitting in every company's existing databases, as the biggest untapped enterprise AI opportunity, bigger than anything language models alone were going to unlock, precisely because so much of what a real business actually runs on lives in exactly that kind of structured record rather than in free text. For a product or delivery leader evaluating AI vendors, this is worth filing away as its own category alongside chatbots and coding assistants: a genuinely new class of AI vendor is emerging around plain structured business data, and it is going to show up in procurement conversations under names and pitches that have nothing to do with language models at all, which means the usual chatbot-focused evaluation checklist will not be the right one to bring to that conversation.

Turn to Uruguay, where the budget fight this briefing has been following all week just got a name attached to it. With the opposition Coalición Republicana, made up of the Blancos, Colorados, and independents, confirming its legislators will not vote for the Rendición de Cuentas at all, describing it as the balance sheet of what National Party senator Javier García called a year of bad government, the ruling Frente Amplio coalition does not have the votes it needs on its own in the Chamber of Deputies. That leaves Cabildo Abierto, a smaller party that has swung the balance of power in this Parliament before, holding the two votes the government actually needs to pass its budget, which turns what looked like a straightforward approval into a real negotiation with a single minor party now holding outsized leverage over a thirty one million dollar spending package it had no hand in designing. This is exactly the same package that passed the Senate on the sixteenth of July and then hit real resistance once it reached the Chamber of Deputies, which this briefing flagged yesterday as worth watching; what has changed in the day since is that the resistance now has a specific number attached to it, two votes, and a specific address, Cabildo Abierto, rather than being a vaguer sense that the opposition would not cooperate. If you manage any kind of approval process that runs through a body where you do not control every vote, this is worth watching as a live example of exactly how a minority holder of the deciding votes extracts concessions: not through public drama or a formal veto, but through the simple, quiet fact of being the last signature a majority actually needs.

That legislative fight is unfolding against a broader economic backdrop that is more sobering than dramatic. Uruguay's own economists are describing 2026 as a year balanced between real macroeconomic stability and an uncomfortably persistent growth slowdown, with output growing at only around two percent, and the single biggest challenge on the table for the rest of the year being a persistent fiscal deficit alongside the need to reactivate investment. The most recent gross domestic product data, covering the third quarter of twenty twenty five, showed one point two percent annual growth but an actual quarter-on-quarter contraction of zero point two percent once seasonal effects are stripped out, which is the kind of quiet, backward-looking number that rarely makes a headline on its own but tends to explain exactly why a government fighting for a thirty one million dollar spending increase is having this much trouble finding the votes for it. Economists covering the country are explicit that macroeconomic stability itself is not in question, inflation and the exchange rate are both behaving, but that stability is coexisting with a persistent fiscal deficit that has to be corrected at some point and a level of private investment that has not picked back up the way planners had hoped. That combination, a government that is fiscally credible in the eyes of markets but structurally short of both growth and votes at the same time, is the actual context behind the Cabildo Abierto negotiation described above: it is not simply a political squabble, it is what a stretched budget looks like once it has to be defended line by line in front of a legislature that is not automatically inclined to say yes.

Now to the story with the most immediate global impact: Iran-aligned Houthi forces in Yemen struck two Saudi oil tankers, named Encelia and Layla, in the Red Sea this week, one of which caught fire, though the crews on both vessels were reported safe, and the group separately declared a maritime blockade against Saudi Arabia entirely. Brent crude, the international oil benchmark, jumped roughly seven percent in response and crossed one hundred dollars a barrel for the first time since May, part of a rise of nearly forty percent for the month as a whole, with at least five tankers reported changing course onto longer, less efficient routes specifically to avoid the area entirely, which by itself adds cost and delay to global shipping well beyond whatever cargo happens to be Saudi-linked. Goldman Sachs is now forecasting Brent could climb above one hundred twenty dollars a barrel by the fourth quarter if the disruption continues, which would be a genuinely different energy-price environment than the one most current planning assumptions were built around. This is landing on top of, not instead of, the Iran story this briefing has followed for over a week: the United States carried out its thirteenth consecutive night of strikes on Iranian targets, hitting command centers, missile and drone launch sites, and air defense systems, and the ministers of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations, meeting in Manila, issued an explicit call for safe and unimpeded passage through the Strait of Hormuz, since the eleven-member bloc depends heavily on Middle Eastern oil and gas and is now watching its own energy security get drawn into a conflict thousands of kilometers away that it has no part in and no ability to influence directly. Put simply, a war that started as a bilateral confrontation between two countries is now visibly repricing energy costs for the entire world, and that repricing is the kind of thing that shows up in a shipping quote or a fuel bill well before it shows up as a resolved or unresolved headline.

The other thread out of that same Manila gathering is Ukraine. Secretary of State Marco Rubio, on the sidelines of his meeting with Russia's Sergei Lavrov, said President Trump was ready to help facilitate an end to the war if the right opportunity arose, while acknowledging real difficulty finding a settlement both Russia and Ukraine could actually accept. That comment landed the same day Ukrainian President Zelenskyy reported that a Russian aerial bomb attack killed three people in the city of Pavlohrad, and Ukraine's air force said Russia had launched a hundred sixty eight attack drones overnight, a scale of nightly drone activity that makes any talk of an imminent settlement hard to square with what is actually happening on the ground while the diplomats talk in a conference room a continent away. This is the fourth Rubio-Lavrov conversation since 2025, following earlier sessions in Riyadh, Kuala Lumpur, and New York, and each one has produced roughly the same kind of statement: both sides describing the conversation as constructive, without either side describing an actual concrete step that changes anything on the battlefield. Read together with yesterday's meeting details and today's casualty count, the honest picture is a peace process that keeps getting kept alive in conversation while the war itself keeps escalating in practice on almost exactly the same timeline, which is a gap worth watching rather than assuming will resolve itself in either direction, and a useful pattern to recognize any time a stalled negotiation of your own keeps generating friendly meetings without producing an actual decision.

That's the briefing. Watch three things this week: whether Cabildo Abierto's price for its two votes changes the actual shape of Uruguay's budget rather than just its headline total, whether Brent crude's climb toward Goldman's one hundred twenty dollar forecast starts showing up in costs you can actually see, from shipping to fuel to anything with a global supply chain, and whether SAP's bet on structured-data AI turns out to be an early signal that other enterprise vendors follow or a one-off, since it is a genuinely different kind of AI story from the model-launch news that usually leads a briefing like this one. There is a common shape underneath today's stories worth naming directly: in each case, a system that looked stable from a distance, an oil market, a legislative majority, an AI market organized around chatbots, turned out to have a single pressure point, a blockade, two missing votes, one company betting on spreadsheets instead of prose, that mattered far more than the headline numbers suggested it would. That is usually a better thing to watch for than the headline itself. Talk soon.
