Good morning. Today's briefing covers three fronts: the AI business is quietly pivoting from selling models to selling implementation, Uruguay's central bank is holding a steady course while a regional trade door opens up, and the Middle East had its sixth straight night of direct US strikes on Iran. Let's get into it.

The biggest AI story this week isn't a new model, it's where the money is actually going. Fortune confirmed that Anthropic has overtaken OpenAI on annualized revenue, tracking toward roughly forty seven billion dollars and now expected to turn profitable a full year ahead of its own guidance. Separately, spend data from the corporate card company Ramp shows Anthropic pulled ahead of OpenAI on business subscriptions back in May, and has held that lead since. But the more interesting move for anyone running delivery work is what both labs are building next to their models, not the models themselves. Anthropic launched a one point five billion dollar joint venture called Ode with Anthropic, alongside the investment firms Blackstone, Hellman and Friedman, and Goldman Sachs, whose entire business is helping large companies actually implement AI inside their existing workflows, rather than just licensing a model and leaving the integration work to the client. OpenAI has now mirrored that move with its own unit, internally called the Deployment Company, which sends AI engineers directly into client offices to do the same kind of hands-on integration work. Read together, this is the clearest signal yet that the real constraint in enterprise AI was never model quality, it was integration labor, and both labs now think that is a bigger, stickier business than the model subscriptions themselves. If you are scoping AI work for a client or for your own organization, expect "implementation partner" to become as normal a line item on a statement of work as the model license itself, and expect the big labs to start competing directly with the consulting firms in that specific line item. On the model side, Anthropic launched Sonnet 5 with performance close to its flagship Opus 4.8 model, at an introductory price of two dollars per million input tokens and ten dollars per million output tokens through the end of August, and separately restored global access to its Fable 5 model after United States export controls that had blocked it were lifted, adding new cybersecurity safeguards in the process. OpenAI answered with GPT-5.6, which had an unusually restricted rollout of only around twenty approved organizations under a Commerce Department review before opening more broadly, with its faster and lighter variants reaching paid and free users within about a day. On the talent side, Anthropic hired Andrej Karpathy, the widely known former Tesla AI director and an OpenAI founding member, along with Tom Blomfield, the co-founder and former chief executive of the digital bank Monzo, who is joining Anthropic's compute team specifically, a hint that securing compute supply, not just model research, is now treated as a board-level priority at these companies. One more data point worth knowing if you are evaluating any of these vendors on safety grounds: the Future of Life Institute's twenty twenty six AI Safety Index gave every frontier lab a grade no higher than a C plus, and Anthropic was the only one to earn even that. For a project manager, the practical takeaway from this whole cluster of news is that the vendor landscape is splitting into two separate purchases: the model itself, which is getting cheaper and more commoditized by the month, and the implementation service around it, which is where the labs, and the consulting firms that compete with them, are now trying to capture the actual margin.

On the ground, the actual day-to-day use of AI by product and project people has become close to universal rather than experimental. Recent surveys put AI usage among product managers at ninety six percent on a frequent basis, with over seventy percent using AI tools daily rather than occasionally. The concrete workflows are worth knowing if you are managing a team that is adopting this, because they are more specific than "using a chatbot." Product managers are using AI agents to continuously monitor competitor releases, pricing changes, app store reviews, and public engineering blogs, then get a standing summary of what changed and why it matters, so competitive intelligence stops being a quarterly slide deck and becomes closer to a live feed. Natural language querying tools now let product managers ask questions directly against product analytics without writing a query language themselves, which changes who can self-serve data instead of waiting in a queue behind a data analyst. And AI-assisted synthesis is compressing what used to be a week of user research analysis into a first pass that is ready in hours, freeing up the actual week for follow-up interviews instead of transcription. The honest read on all of this, and the one worth repeating to your own team, is that it is a role shift, not a role replacement: as the mechanical parts of the job, drafting, first-pass analysis, backlog triage, get cheaper to produce, the scarce skill left is judgment, meaning deciding what should exist and why, rather than producing the artifact once somebody has already decided. That has a direct consequence for how you manage a delivery team today: code review, estimation, and quality assurance all need to assume that a meaningful share of the output in front of you was drafted by an assistant rather than typed from scratch, which changes what a reviewer should actually be checking for, and shifts more of the real risk toward the review step than toward the drafting step.

Uruguay's central bank kept its policy rate unchanged at five point seven five percent this month, and the decision was unanimous across its monetary policy committee. Inflation ticked up to three point eight percent in May, but the bank held its position because two-year inflation expectations have stayed anchored right around its four point five percent target, and the latest survey of analysts and consulting firms puts those expectations at exactly that figure. Growth is the softer part of the picture: gross domestic product is now expected to expand only around one point nine percent this year, down from the more optimistic two point five percent that forecasters had been projecting for twenty twenty six as recently as last July, a meaningful downgrade over just twelve months. That is worth watching closely if your own planning assumes a stronger domestic market than that, particularly for anything consumer-facing. Separately, new labor categories for domestic workers, General, Kitchen, and Care, took effect on the first of July, with the national social security bank initially placing all currently active workers into the General category by default, something worth flagging if this touches your own household or a client's local operations. The central bank's next policy meeting is set for the eighteenth of August, and given this growth downgrade, that meeting is worth watching for any change in tone even without a change in the rate itself. Taken together, the picture for Uruguay right now is a central bank successfully keeping inflation expectations anchored while growth quietly cools underneath it, which is the kind of combination that tends to show up first in softer consumer spending rather than in any single dramatic headline.

There is a regional trade story worth pairing with that economic caution, because it points the other direction. Chile's president, José Antonio Kast, used a recent visit to voice explicit support for bringing Uruguay into the Bioceanic Corridor, the overland trade route connecting Atlantic and Pacific ports across the southern half of South America, and confirmed Chilean backing for Uruguay's bid to join the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership, the major Pacific trade bloc usually referred to by its acronym, C P T P P. For a small, trade-dependent economy like Uruguay's, that kind of explicit backing from a neighboring government matters more than it might first appear, since accession to blocs like this one typically needs support from existing members just to get onto the agenda, and Chile is already inside the bloc. If Uruguay's accession talks do advance this year, it would sit alongside a wider wave of countries moving in the same direction, which is exactly the trade realignment described later in this briefing, and it would give a small, open economy like Uruguay's a second major market bloc to lean on beyond its immediate South American neighbors.

The Middle East had its sixth consecutive night of direct United States strikes on Iranian targets, including strikes described as hitting civilian infrastructure, escalating a conflict that many had hoped a prior ceasefire had closed off for good. Iran's Revolutionary Guard claimed responsibility for retaliatory strikes against American military assets spread across Bahrain, Kuwait, Oman, Qatar, Jordan, and Syria, meaning this is no longer a contained two-country exchange but one now touching six other states that host American forces on their soil. A NATO summit held in Turkey this week was supposed to address the alliance's overall posture, but reportedly left open the bigger question of what role the United States intends to keep playing in European defense while it is this deep into a separate Middle East confrontation. Germany's government separately warned of a looming "security vacuum" in southern Lebanon once the United Nations peacekeeping mandate there, known as UNIFIL, expires at the end of this year. And Israel is now heading toward elections on the twenty seventh of October, after its parliament, the Knesset, dissolved following its first full term since nineteen eighty eight, with clashes also breaking out after settlers attacked the outskirts of a West Bank town called Sinjil. None of these threads are resolved, and together they point to a genuinely unsettled second half of the year for anyone with business or travel exposure to the region, whether that is a client with regional operations, supply chains that route through Gulf shipping lanes, or simply energy prices that tend to move on exactly this kind of news.

Global trade rules shifted materially this month too, and the throughline connects straight back to Uruguay's own trade ambitions. A negotiated deal between the European Union and the United States took effect on the first of July: the European Union is removing its duties on American industrial goods and giving preferential access to some American seafood and farm products, while in exchange, most European Union exports into the United States now face a flat fifteen percent tariff going forward. The European Union also closed a loophole that same day, ending duty-free treatment for parcels under one hundred fifty euros and rolling out a new import declaration system, called I C S 2, that now covers the large majority of cross-border online shopping into the bloc. On the American side, a separate ten percent global import surcharge imposed under a different legal authority is set to expire this month, while tariffs of one hundred percent on patented pharmaceutical ingredients are being phased in, starting with large companies this month and extending to every other producer in September, a detail worth knowing if any client sits in that supply chain. Meanwhile, Indonesia, the Philippines, and the United Arab Emirates have all opened talks to join that same Pacific trade bloc that Uruguay is now eyeing, which tells you the bloc is actively expanding right now rather than sitting still, so the timing of Uruguay's own push, and Chile's backing for it, matters more than it would in a quieter year for global trade. For anyone doing planning that assumes trade rules stay stable for the next twelve months, this month alone is a reminder that they are not: tariff rates, parcel exemptions, and bloc membership are all moving at once, in different directions, across different regions.

That is the briefing. Watch three things this week: whether Anthropic and OpenAI's push into implementation services starts showing up in vendor pitches you are personally fielding, whether the Iran situation draws in a seventh night or starts to cool off, and whether Uruguay's central bank signals anything new about growth at its August meeting given this month's downgrade. None of these three stories are really separate from each other once you sit with them for a moment: money, trade rules, and geopolitical risk are all repricing at the same time this month, and a small open economy sitting between larger blocs, much like the position an individual project manager sits in between a client and a delivery team, tends to feel that kind of repricing before the headlines fully catch up to it. Talk soon.
